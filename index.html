The Price of a Dream

From Middle-Class Lives in China to Low-Wage Labor in the U.S., “Walk-Line” Migrants Question What Was Gained — and Lost.

By Iris Qiu and Edison Wu

A t 5 a.m., Denvy Jiang’s alarm buzzes to life. Outside, the streets are still cloaked in darkness. In the next room, his wife and two children remain fast asleep.

Jiang rises quietly, careful not to wake his family or the tenants behind the four other doors in the crowded shared house. He wolfs down a few bites of the breakfast his wife set out the night before, grabs his car keys and slips into his secondhand Honda. Minutes later, he’s merging onto the highway, bound for a delivery warehouse nearly 20 miles away.

It’s his 257th day in the United States.

Just over six months ago, Jiang and his wife left their jobs in Shanghai, China’s largest city and a global financial hub, where they had spent more than a decade working as private home tutors. Together, they earned more than 40,000 yuan a month (about $5,600), enough to live comfortably in the city.

They sold their house and car, then brought their young son and daughter across the Pacific. Under the cover of night, they crossed the U.S.-Mexico border, scaling a barbed-wire fence and stepping into a country they had only seen in Hollywood movies.

Now, the family lives in Los Angeles. Each day, Jiang spends up to 14 hours on the road, delivering nearly 200 packages.

He is just one of tens of thousands of Chinese immigrants who, in the wake of the pandemic, took the uncertain and costly route to the United States, hoping to plant new roots in unfamiliar soil.
